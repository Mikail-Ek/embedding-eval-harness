An embedding is a way of turning a piece of text into a list of numbers, called a vector, that captures its meaning rather than just its literal words. The core idea is that pieces of text with similar meaning end up with similar vectors, even when the actual wording is completely different. For example, the sentences "Did I miss a call from support?" and "How do I reach customer service?" share almost no words in common, but an embedding model would represent them with vectors that are close together, because they both mean the same thing.

This property is what makes semantic search possible. Semantic search means finding relevant results by matching meaning rather than matching exact keywords. To do this, every piece of text in a collection of documents is converted into a vector once, and stored. When a user submits a query, that query is converted into a vector using the same embedding model, and the system searches for the documents whose vectors are closest to the query's vector.

There are different types of embedding models with different trade-offs. Attention-based models compute a fresh, context-aware vector for each piece of text by considering every word in relation to every other word, which is accurate but computationally expensive. Static embedding models instead look up a fixed vector for each word from a table and average them together, which is much faster but loses the ability to understand context. Choosing between them in production involves balancing retrieval quality against speed, cost, and hardware requirements.
